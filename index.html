I am a PhD student and NSF-funded Graduate Student Research Assistant in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the University of Michigan-Dearborn, where I work in the Information Systems, Security, and Forensics (ISSF) Laboratory with Prof. Hafiz Malik.

My research is on securing cyber-physical systems at the hardware level. Modern vehicles and connected devices contain dozens of embedded computers, and the assumption that each one is genuine and untampered with is rarely verified. I build systems that check that assumption directly — using the physical characteristics of the silicon itself.

Specifically, I work on:

  • Hardware fingerprinting — identifying devices by manufacturing variations unique to each chip
  • FPGA-based intrusion detection — detection that runs in real time, in the vehicle, rather than in the cloud
  • Side-channel analysis — using electromagnetic and power measurements to detect tampering and counterfeit components

Before starting my PhD I spent two years as an FPGA engineer, building high-performance digital systems in production. I received my BS in Electrical Engineering from the University of Engineering and Technology, Peshawar, graduating as a gold medalist.

Research in Action

From bench to conference floor

A device does not need to announce what it is. Manufacturing leaves every chip with slightly different switching behaviour — overshoot, ringing, settling time. An attacker can copy firmware, a serial number, or a message. They cannot copy physics.

  1. 01 · The mechanism

    Physics as identity

    Two chips off the same reel switch slightly differently. That difference is measurable, and it is not something firmware can rewrite.

  2. 02 · The bench

    Same make, same model

    Eight devices across four hardware types, including boards of the same make and model.

    99.5% real-time accuracy — no firmware changes, no protocol access

  3. 03 · Production hardware

    Nine real ECUs, weeks apart

    Electrically identical production automotive ECUs, captured across thirteen sessions over several weeks. A fingerprint that holds only within one sitting cannot be deployed.

  4. 04 · Baltimore

    Live on the table

    Real devices at VehicleSec ’26, identified in real time purely from the analog shape of their switching signals.

    A hardware fingerprinting framework for authenticating embedded devices in vehicular networks. The approach analyses the analog characteristics of digital switching behaviour to detect compromised or counterfeit devices, extracting 211 waveform features and applying Random Forest and stacked ensemble methods to achieve 99.5% accuracy under controlled conditions and 100% device type classification across the tested hardware.
